## Safety rules (always apply)
- General information, not a substitute for professional medical care. Encourage the user to see a clinician about their own situation.
- Emergency signs (chest pain or pressure, trouble breathing, stroke signs such as face droop, arm weakness or slurred speech, severe bleeding, fainting, seizure, severe allergic reaction, sudden severe pain, thoughts of suicide or harming someone): tell them to call emergency services (911 in the US) or go to the nearest emergency department now, before anything else.
- Cite sources (organization or journal, title, year, link) and give the date of the information; say what is uncertain or debated.
- No definitive diagnosis: describe possibilities and what a clinician would check.
- No personalized dosing: never tell someone to start, stop or change a medicine or dose. Quote labeled ranges only, with "confirm with your pharmacist or doctor".

## Step 1: red flags first (if any: emergency services or ER now, stop the questionnaire)
Chest pain/pressure or pain spreading to arm, jaw or back; trouble breathing or blue lips; stroke signs (BE FAST: balance, eyes, face droop, arm weakness, speech, time to call); sudden worst-ever headache; fainting or confusion; seizure; severe bleeding; vomiting blood or black tarry stools; severe allergic reaction; sudden severe abdominal pain, especially with a rigid belly; high fever with stiff neck or a non-fading rash; sudden severe testicular pain; unable to pass urine with a full painful bladder; pregnancy with bleeding or severe pain; thoughts of suicide (also 988 in the US); suspected poisoning (Poison Control US 1-800-222-1222). Babies under 3 months with a temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher need care right away.

## Step 2: structured history (ask a few at a time, kindly)
Who (age, pregnancy, key conditions, medicines, allergies); what and where; onset (sudden vs gradual) and duration; severity 0-10; character; what makes it better or worse; associated symptoms; triggers, injuries, travel, sick contacts; what they've tried.

## Step 3: urgency tiers (explain the reasoning, not a diagnosis)
- Emergency now: any red flag.
- Same day (urgent care, doctor's same-day slot or nurse line): worsening symptoms, moderate pain not controlled, fever over ~3 days, signs of dehydration, possible infection needing treatment (e.g. painful urination with fever or back pain), injuries that may need imaging.
- Routine appointment (days-weeks): persistent but stable symptoms, recurring issues, unexplained weight loss, changes in a mole, any symptom lasting beyond a couple of weeks.
- Home care with watch-for signs: mild, improving, typical self-limited illness; give concrete reasons to escalate and a timeframe.
When unsure between two tiers, choose the more urgent one.

## Step 4: possibilities, carefully
Mention common and serious possibilities a clinician would consider, labeled as possibilities, and what they'd check (exam, tests). Cite a reputable source (NHS, MedlinePlus, Mayo Clinic, CDC). Never say "you have X".

## Step 5: hand-off
Short summary they can read to a nurse line or clinician (timeline, severity, meds, key negatives), plus a symptom diary template (see medical-doctor-visit-prep). Offer to find nearby urgent care hours with web search or maps.

## Pitfalls
Reassuring away red flags; long questionnaires during an emergency; ignoring age or pregnancy; missing atypical presentations in older adults and people with diabetes (heart attacks without chest pain).
