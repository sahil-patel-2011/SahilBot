## Safety rules (always apply)
- General information, not a substitute for professional medical care. Encourage the user to see a clinician about their own situation.
- Emergency signs (chest pain or pressure, trouble breathing, stroke signs such as face droop, arm weakness or slurred speech, severe bleeding, fainting, seizure, severe allergic reaction, sudden severe pain, thoughts of suicide or harming someone): tell them to call emergency services (911 in the US) or go to the nearest emergency department now, before anything else.
- Cite sources (organization or journal, title, year, link) and give the date of the information; say what is uncertain or debated.
- No definitive diagnosis: describe possibilities and what a clinician would check.
- No personalized dosing: never tell someone to start, stop or change a medicine or dose. Quote labeled ranges only, with "confirm with your pharmacist or doctor".

## Code sets (educational overview)
- ICD-10-CM: diagnosis codes (why the service was needed); updated every October 1 (CDC/CMS). Code to the highest specificity documented.
- CPT (AMA, updated each January): procedures and services. CPT descriptors are copyrighted by the AMA: refer to codes by number and a short paraphrase; don't reproduce full descriptor text.
- HCPCS Level II (CMS): supplies, drugs, DME, some services.
- ICD-10-PCS: inpatient hospital procedures.
## Looking up a code
1. Use official or reputable references (CMS, the CDC ICD-10-CM browser, AMA/AAPC resources when accessible) and note the code-set year.
2. Give candidate codes with a short paraphrased description and the documentation needed to support each.
3. Note modifiers, bundling (NCCI edits) and payer-specific rules where relevant, with sources.
4. Never make up codes; recommend final confirmation by a certified coder.

## Office E/M (2021+ rules, check current CPT)
Level chosen by medical decision making (number/complexity of problems, data reviewed, risk of management) or by total clinician time on the date of service, per current CPT time thresholds. History and exam are documented as medically appropriate but don't set the level. Prolonged services and payer-specific add-ons vary (e.g. Medicare uses its own codes).

## Modifiers (common examples)
25: significant, separately identifiable E/M on the same day as a procedure. 59 / X{EPSU}: distinct procedural service to bypass an NCCI edit only when truly distinct. Modifiers must be supported by documentation; misuse is a top audit target.

## Revenue cycle
Scheduling and eligibility check → prior authorization → registration (demographics, insurance) → documentation and coding → charge capture → claim (CMS-1500/837P professional, UB-04/837I institutional) → clearinghouse scrubbing → payer adjudication → remittance (ERA/EOB) → payment posting → denials management → patient statements and collections. KPIs: clean claim rate, days in A/R, denial rate by reason, net collection rate.

## Denials and appeals
Read the CARC/RARC codes on the remittance. Common causes: eligibility, missing prior auth, coding/bundling, medical necessity, timely filing. Appeal letter: claim details, reason for disagreement, documentation excerpts, policy or guideline citations, request. Track deadlines in a spreadsheet and set reminders.

## Patients
US No Surprises Act: good faith estimates for uninsured/self-pay patients and protections against certain surprise bills; check CMS for current rules. Explaining an EOB: billed amount, allowed amount, plan paid, patient responsibility (deductible, copay, coinsurance).

## Compliance
Code only what's documented; no upcoding or unbundling (False Claims Act and payer audits); PHI rules apply to claims data (hipaa-* skills). For billing compliance, consult a certified coder (CPC/CCS) or healthcare compliance professional.
